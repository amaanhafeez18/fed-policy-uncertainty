How uncertain does the Fed sound? A weekly index read by three AI models, 1994–2015

Weekly index from December 31, 1993 to May 29, 2015 · 1,118 weeks · Amaan Hafeez

Three AI language models scored 40,039 paragraphs from 1,518 Federal Reserve documents for how uncertain the Fed sounds about its own policy path. They largely agree on which paragraphs are about policy, but their weekly indices barely move with established uncertainty measures: across 12 comparisons with four such measures, the largest correlation of changes is 0.13, and 2 pass the 5% cut-off.

2 · Cleaning

How the text was cleaned

  1. Read each page. Only the document's own text, without menus, page headers or links.
  2. Cut it into paragraphs at the Fed's own paragraph breaks, 60 to 250 words each. Short pieces are joined to the next one and long ones are split at the end of a sentence.
  3. Remove what is not the Fed speaking: attendance lists, vote tallies, bibliographies, headings and datelines, and reporters' questions at press conferences.
  4. Keep everything else, including footnotes, numbered points, bullet lists and the policy directive quoted in the minutes.

3 · The prompt

What each model was asked

Every paragraph goes to each model on its own, with no date, no speaker and no document name. All three models get the same instructions and answer in a fixed format with five fields.

policy_relevant
Yes or no: does the paragraph say anything about the future path of U.S. monetary policy (the federal funds rate, the balance sheet, or how the Fed will decide)?
policy_uncertainty
0 to 10: how uncertain the Fed sounds about its own future policy path. A paragraph that is not about policy scores 0. The index is built from this number.
economic_uncertainty
0 to 10: how uncertain it sounds about the economic outlook.
stance
Hawkish, neutral or dovish, kept separate so a firm commitment is not mistaken for certainty or uncertainty.
reason
At most 25 words, quoting the phrase that drove the score.
0A definite, pre-committed path
2–3A clear direction with mild conditioning
5Direction known; pace or endpoint open and data dependent
7–8The path is highly uncertain; several scenarios possible
10The Fed says it does not know what it will do
Full instructions, word for word 
You are a research assistant scoring Federal Reserve communications for an economics study on monetary-policy uncertainty. You will see one passage at a time, taken from an FOMC statement, FOMC minutes, a press conference, or a speech by a Fed official. You are not told its date or speaker. Judge only what the passage itself says. Do not use any knowledge of later events or of how markets reacted.

Score the passage on these fields:

1. policy_relevant (true/false): true if the passage says anything about the FUTURE path of U.S. monetary policy: the federal funds rate, the balance sheet (asset purchases or runoff), or how the Fed will decide (forward guidance, data dependence, conditions for changing course). False for topics such as bank supervision, payments, climate, financial inclusion, or a description of past data with no link to upcoming policy.

2. policy_uncertainty (integer 0-10): how uncertain the passage signals the Fed is about ITS OWN FUTURE POLICY PATH (the pace, size, timing, or endpoint of rate changes or balance-sheet changes).
   0 = a definite, pre-committed path ("we will raise the rate by 50 basis points at each of the next two meetings").
   2-3 = a clear direction with mild conditioning ("ongoing increases will be appropriate").
   5 = the direction is known but the pace or endpoint is explicitly open and data dependent.
   7-8 = explicit statements that the path is highly uncertain, several scenarios are possible, or policy could move in different directions.
   10 = the Fed says it genuinely does not know what it will do, or the passage is extremely ambiguous about policy.
   If policy_relevant is false, give 0.

3. economic_uncertainty (integer 0-10): how much uncertainty the passage expresses about the economic outlook (inflation, growth, the labor market, geopolitical events, financial conditions), using the same 0-10 anchors.

4. stance ("hawkish", "neutral", or "dovish"): which way the passage leans on policy. Hawkish means leaning toward tighter policy (higher rates, faster runoff). Dovish means leaning toward easier policy. Use neutral when it leans neither way or is not about policy.

5. reason: at most 25 words, quoting or naming the phrase that drove your policy_uncertainty score.

Stance and uncertainty are different things. "We will keep raising rates until the job is done" is hawkish with LOW uncertainty. "We may slow the pace, or we may need to go further; it will depend on the data" has HIGH uncertainty whatever its stance. Hedged but routine Fed language ("the Committee will continue to monitor") should not be scored as high uncertainty on its own.

From paragraphs to a weekly index. For each model, the policy-uncertainty scores of the paragraphs it calls about policy are averaged within each document, then across the documents released in each week (weeks end on Friday). A week with no new policy text keeps the previous week's value.

7 · Against other measures

Each model against established measures and the economy

If the index captures uncertainty about Fed policy, it should move with other measures of it. The four uncertainty measures are the Husted–Rogers–Sun newspaper index of uncertainty about Fed policy, the Baker–Bloom–Davis monetary-policy index, the VIX and the volatility of the 2-year Treasury yield. Inflation and unemployment show how the index relates to the economy. Choose a measure and a model.

GPT-6 LunaMeasure

Both lines are in standard deviations from their own average so they share one scale, and smoothed (13 weeks or 3 months) to be readable; the correlations use the unsmoothed values. Levels ask whether the index is high when the measure is high. Changes ask whether it rises when the measure rises, the stricter test, since two series that trend over 21 years correlate in levels even when unrelated. Monthly comparisons use the monthly average of the weekly index.
